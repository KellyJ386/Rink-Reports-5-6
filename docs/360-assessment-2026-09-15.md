# Rink Reports — 360° application assessment

**Assessment date:** 2026-09-15  
**Scope:** application architecture, product experience, security and tenant isolation, data
integrity, operations, delivery controls, offline behavior, and the interaction between Employee
Scheduling and Rink/Facility Scheduling.  
**Method:** repository review plus local lint, unit-test, type-check, and cron-manifest checks. This
was not a production-data review, a live usability study, a penetration test, or a fully seeded
browser test.

## Executive verdict

Rink Reports is a broad, unusually mature vertical operations product rather than a collection of
forms. Its strongest qualities are its facility-scoped authorization model, database-enforced
scheduling rules, append-oriented audit strategy, offline submission support, and a serious set of
operational modules. The current architecture can support a controlled production rollout.

The largest product risk is now **coordination, not missing CRUD**. Employee Scheduling answers
“who is working?” while Rink Scheduling answers “what is happening on the ice?” They are connected
only through a deliberately one-way coverage check. That catches an unstaffed booking, but it does
not tell a particular qualified employee what work the booking creates, verify that the work was
acknowledged, or link completion evidence back to the booking. The next major investment should be
a small orchestration/task layer, not more independent module features.

The largest immediate engineering concern found in this pass was duplicated mapping keys in three
GitHub Actions workflows (`uses` and `node-version`). Even if a YAML consumer accepts the files by
silently taking the last value, the workflows are ambiguous and may be rejected by stricter
parsers. This assessment's companion change removes those merge artifacts; the updated workflows
must complete successfully before CI is relied on as a release gate.

## What is working well

### Product and information architecture

- The product covers the operating day end to end: staff, permissions, daily reports, incidents,
  accidents, ice and refrigeration operations, communications, analytics, employee rosters, ice
  bookings, contracts, invoicing, locker rooms, resurfacing, and public/display surfaces.
- Admin and staff experiences are separated, and module permissions—not hardcoded role names—are
  used in many important paths. That is the right model for facilities whose job titles vary.
- Facility timezone handling is a first-class concern in recent scheduling views and validation.
- Existing review/runbook documentation is strong enough to support handoff to another engineer or
  operator. The gap is consolidating it into one current source of truth.

### Security and correctness

- Employee assignment rules converge on database validation rather than relying only on UI checks.
  The implementation covers publishing, open-shift claims, swaps, availability, time off,
  certification, rest, overtime, minors, and double-booking.
- Published shifts are locked behind an explicit governance process. The rink coverage reader also
  filters specifically on `status = 'published'`, so drafts and cancelled shifts cannot create a
  false impression of coverage.
- Sensitive recurring work uses authenticated cron wrappers, service-role clients, durable queues,
  and run records. Security-sensitive schema behavior has dedicated SQL harnesses and CI workflows.
- There are no `as any`, `@ts-ignore`, `@ts-expect-error`, `TODO`, `FIXME`, or `HACK` markers in the
  TypeScript application at the time of this review. That is a useful maintainability signal, not a
  substitute for behavioral tests.

### Engineering structure

- Pure business logic is regularly separated from database reads and React components, which makes
  coverage, calendars, rates, permissions, retry behavior, and schedule models unit-testable.
- The repository has 113 unit/E2E test files and dedicated E2E scenarios for scheduling, rink
  scheduling, permissions, multi-tenancy, admin workflows, and operational reports.
- Generated database types and a schema snapshot reduce drift between migrations and application
  code. Migration-prefix and RLS-isolation workflows show appropriate caution around a large SQL
  history.

## How Employee Scheduling and Facility Scheduling interact today

### Current contract

The two modules have separate sources of truth:

- `schedule_shifts` owns the employee roster. Only assigned, **published** shifts count as staffing.
- `rink_bookings` owns facility/ice demand. `blocks_until`, rather than only the customer booking end,
  includes the post-booking resurfacing buffer.
- Facility operating hours and date exceptions are an independent constraint on booking coverage.

Rink Scheduling evaluates every future live booking against two questions:

1. Is the complete booking-plus-buffer window contained within facility operating hours?
2. Does one assigned published employee shift contain that complete window?

The result is stored on the booking as `covered`, `gap_hours`, `gap_staffing`, or `gap_both`.
Changes can open or resolve a communication alert and notify users with relevant scheduling
permissions. A `schedule_shifts` database trigger debounces changes into
`rink_coverage_reeval_queue`, which is drained by the coverage cron. Booking and settings actions
also enqueue re-evaluation. Importantly, the integration reads Employee Scheduling and writes only
Rink Scheduling/communications records; it cannot mutate or block an employee roster write.

### Why this design is sound

- It keeps ownership boundaries clear and prevents a booking engine from silently changing a labor
  schedule.
- It is eventually consistent without running a full 120-day scan once per row in a multi-shift
  publish.
- Using one containing shift is conservative and makes responsibility unambiguous across handoffs.
- Coverage is advisory: a booking outside hours or without staff is visible but can still be entered,
  which matches real front-desk operations better than a hard block.
- The booking buffer is correctly included in both operating-hours and staffing coverage.

### Where the interaction stops short

1. **Coverage means “a person is present,” not “the right person is present.”** The sweep loads only
   shift times. It does not consider the shift's job area, employee qualification, certification,
   department, or whether an ice technician/duty manager is required for the booking type.
2. **There is no explicit demand model.** A busy event, public session, tournament, or simultaneous
   multi-rink use may require several roles and headcounts. A single generic published shift marks a
   booking covered.
3. **There is no assignment of work.** Resurfacing, rental start/end, locker-room turnover, setup,
   teardown, and compliance readings are not durable tasks assigned to the rostered person.
4. **There is no acknowledgement or completion loop.** An alert tells a manager that coverage is
   missing, but a covered booking does not prove that a responsible employee saw or completed the
   expected work.
5. **The user must bridge two screens.** The coverage alert explains that the manager can adjust the
   rink schedule or roster cover, but there is no linked split view, proposed employee, or
   one-workflow resolution.
6. **A failed enqueue is intentionally swallowed.** This prevents facility coverage bookkeeping from
   breaking a roster publish, but it also makes scheduled fallback sweeps and queue/cron monitoring
   essential.
7. **Coverage is facility-wide.** The current rule does not establish that an employee is assigned to
   the relevant rink/space or can cover concurrent operational obligations.

### Recommended target state

Keep both schedules authoritative and add a third, derived layer:

```text
Facility booking/resurface ─┐
                            ├─> operational event/rule ─> task ─> acknowledgement/completion
Published employee shift ───┘                          └─> exception/escalation
```

Do not directly synchronize the two source tables. Instead:

- define demand rules by booking type, rink/space, relative time window, required job area or
  certification, minimum headcount, and escalation policy;
- match only published shifts that overlap the full task window and meet those requirements;
- create idempotent, durable tasks with source links, an assignment reason, due time, state, and
  immutable activity history;
- connect task completion to an existing ice-operations, cleaning, refrigeration, or daily-report
  submission rather than duplicating its data; and
- show exceptions early during booking and roster planning, then activate tasks only when their
  source booking and employee shift are committed.

The existing `docs/operations-schedule-orchestration-plan.md` is the right detailed design. Its
recommended shadow-mode pilot should be treated as the next product phase, subject to the product
questions below.

## Findings and advised changes

### P0 — before treating CI or a deployment as trusted

1. **Verify the repaired GitHub Actions workflows.** `.github/workflows/ci.yml`,
   `.github/workflows/rls-isolation.yml`, and
   `.github/workflows/regenerate-migration-artifacts.yml` each declared two `uses` keys in one step
   and two `node-version` keys in another. This change keeps pnpm/action-setup v5 and Node 22, matching
   the package's supported runtime and `.node-version`. Confirm all three in GitHub, then add a
   workflow lint step (for example, `actionlint`) so the merge artifact cannot recur.
2. **Run the full release gates in the intended Node runtime.** The local environment in this review
   uses Node 20 while the project requires Node 22 or newer. Local results are valuable but should
   not replace a clean Node 22/24 CI run.
3. **Run seeded E2E for both schedule modules together.** The critical journey is: create/confirm a
   booking, publish an insufficient roster, observe a gap, publish coverage, observe resolution,
   then edit/cancel the shift and observe the gap reopen. Include timezone and post-booking-buffer
   boundaries.

### P1 — next release cycle

1. **Make coverage qualification-aware before automating tasks.** Add required job-area/headcount
   rules per booking type, with a safe default of the current “one on-duty employee” behavior. Show
   the exact missing role, not only “no published shift.”
2. **Create a unified resolution view.** From a coverage alert, open the booking beside candidate
   published shifts/open shifts, explain why each candidate qualifies, and allow the user to create
   a draft shift or adjust the booking. Preserve two-person publish approval.
3. **Add coverage freshness and ownership to the UI.** Display `coverage_checked_at`, the rule used,
   and whether a gap is awaiting roster publication or cron evaluation. Assign every open gap to a
   named manager or queue, with acknowledgement and aging.
4. **Strengthen sweep failure semantics.** Queue rows are marked done before processing and the
   design relies on the recurring full sweep/new changes for retry. Record a failed state, attempt
   count, and last error, or atomically claim as `processing` and transition to `done` only after a
   successful facility sweep. Keep a dead-letter/alert threshold.
5. **Consolidate scheduling notifications.** Staff scheduling notifications and communication alerts
   are separate inbox concepts. Give users one inbox and preference model even if the backing event
   tables remain specialized.
6. **Establish operational SLOs.** Suggested starting values: 99% of booking/shift changes evaluated
   within 10 minutes; no unresolved staffing gap inside 24 hours of booking start; no orphaned
   processing queue row older than 15 minutes; notification delivery failures visible to an admin.

### P2 — product differentiation

1. Implement the orchestration pilot for ice-make preparation, rental start/end awareness,
   locker-room turnover, and once-per-shift refrigeration work.
2. Add “suggest fill” ranking based on qualification, availability/preference, rest/overtime risk,
   task load, and cost—while keeping the existing database validator authoritative.
3. Build a combined forecast: bookings by hour, required staffing by role, scheduled staffing,
   exceptions, and estimated labor. This is more valuable to a rink manager than independent
   calendars.
4. Add schedule-change impact previews: bookings that will lose coverage, tasks that will be
   reassigned/cancelled, and employees who will be notified before an approver confirms publication.
5. Measure outcomes, not feature use: prevented uncovered rentals, task on-time rate, schedule-change
   acknowledgement, open-shift fill time, overtime avoided, and false-positive coverage alerts.

## Cross-cutting assessment

### UX and accessibility

The UI system is coherent and staff pages appear intentionally mobile-first. The main UX risk is the
number of modules and parallel inboxes, not basic visual inconsistency. Conduct scenario-based tests
with a front-desk employee, ice technician, scheduling manager, and general manager. Test at 320 px,
keyboard only, 200% zoom, dark mode, and intermittent connectivity. Prioritize task-oriented language
(“Rental uncovered at 7:00 PM”) over module-oriented language (“gap_staffing”).

### Data, privacy, and retention

The data model contains employee, injury/incident, customer, billing, and operational records with
different sensitivity and retention needs. Confirm facility-specific retention and export/deletion
policy with counsel and operators. Ensure audit records retain actor/reason without retaining more
medical or customer detail than needed. Continue testing every service-role job with explicit
facility predicates because service role bypasses RLS.

### Performance and scale

The 120-day facility sweep is reasonable for a small pilot but grows with bookings, facilities, and
change frequency. Capture query timings and rows evaluated before optimizing. If needed, evolve from
full facility sweeps to dirty time windows while retaining a periodic full reconciliation. Paginate
all administrative history/list views and define realistic upper bounds for employees, simultaneous
rinks, recurring series, and bookings per facility.

### Reliability and operations

Queues, idempotency keys, alert uniqueness, cron authorization, and cron run logging are the right
building blocks. Add dashboards for queue age, sweep duration/error, notification age/failure,
coverage gaps by start proximity, offline replay failure, and schema/migration drift. Every cron job
should have an owner, an expected cadence, a stale threshold, and a documented manual replay path.

### Testing strategy

Keep the fast pure-function tests, SQL/RLS harnesses, and type/schema freshness gates. Add fewer but
deeper cross-module acceptance tests with clock control. The most important missing confidence is not
another isolated page test; it is proving that database trigger → queue → cron → booking status →
alert lifecycle works under publish, cancellation, failure, concurrency, and DST boundaries.

## Questions for product and operations

These answers materially affect the design and should be resolved before building orchestration:

1. What does “facility schedule” mean to your operators: ice bookings only, all bookable spaces,
   opening hours, a public calendar, or one combined master calendar?
2. For each booking type, which roles, certifications, and minimum headcounts are mandatory? Do
   requirements change by attendance, age group, alcohol service, tournament status, or rink count?
3. Must one employee cover the complete booking-plus-resurface window, or are documented handoffs
   acceptable? If handoffs are allowed, what overlap is required?
4. Does an on-duty employee cover the whole facility, one rink, or a specific zone? How should two
   simultaneous rinks compete for one qualified employee?
5. Is the resurfacing buffer always staff-demand time, and must it specifically be covered by an ice
   technician rather than any employee?
6. Which system is authoritative when a contract/customer commitment conflicts with staffing:
   should booking remain allowed with escalation, require manager override, or be blocked?
7. Who owns an uncovered booking: booking creator, scheduling manager, duty manager, general
   manager, or a configurable escalation chain?
8. How far ahead should coverage become actionable, and how quickly must same-day changes notify
   staff and managers?
9. Which actions require explicit acknowledgement? Is “seen” enough, or must the employee accept
   responsibility for the task?
10. What evidence closes each task—tap complete, checklist, note, photo, machine selection, or an
    existing report submission?
11. Can staff decline or hand off assigned operational tasks? Who approves reassignment?
12. Should task assignment prefer fairness, least overtime, lowest cost, direct job-area match,
    continuity with the booking, or a facility-configurable priority order?
13. Are employee wage rates in scope for scheduling forecasts, and who may view them?
14. Should customers or public displays ever see staffing/coverage status? The recommendation is no;
    expose schedule changes, never internal labor gaps.
15. What is the offline expectation for the rink floor: read-only schedule, task acknowledgement,
    full completion/report capture, or all of these?
16. What are the actual facility scale bounds—employees, rinks/spaces, bookings per day, seasonal
    templates, and number of facilities per organization?
17. Which existing reports are legally/compliance required, at what cadence, and what late/missed
    escalation is required?
18. Who will own daily monitoring after launch, and what response time can that person realistically
    provide for failed cron jobs, delivery failures, or stuck offline submissions?

## Recommended decision

Proceed with a **limited, instrumented pilot** after repairing CI workflow syntax and completing the
cross-schedule E2E journey. Keep the present coverage integration advisory. In parallel, answer the
role/headcount/handoff questions and run the proposed orchestration layer in shadow mode at one rink
for two weeks. Promote only workflows whose suggestions operators judge accurate, and do so one
workflow at a time.
